There's a popular answer to this, and you've probably heard it: It's dopamine. Social media hacks your brain's reward system.

It's not wrong, exactly. There's a real mechanism behind it: rewards that arrive unpredictably produce a stronger neurological response than rewards that arrive on schedule. It's the same principle behind a slot machine, and it's been measured directly — in one PET imaging study, unpredictable monetary rewards triggered a release of dopamine in the brain's reward circuitry that a fixed, predictable payout of the same size did not.

So the mechanism is real.

But "dopamine" as an explanation has a problem: it makes the feed sound like a drug, and you the passive victim of your own brain chemistry. That's satisfying to say. It's also too simple to be useful — because it skips the part you can actually see and name: the design of the thing you're holding.

So let's ask a more specific question.

Not "why does your brain like rewards" — every brain does.

But: what, specifically, about this feed, makes it so hard to find a place to stop?
